a queue for your attention

Don’t hold the work
in your head.

Your head is for the work itself. The queue holds everything else, so you are not rehearsing a list while you try to focus. mm shows one thing. If something more important comes up, you put it on the stack. It goes first. When you finish it, you are back on the queue.

  • Now one thing, no picking mm
  • Stack something just became more important mm add -p "prod is down"
  • Quick a small nag, kept off the line mm add -q "email Sam"

Three places

        

          

            
Queue

            
The line of real work. First thing you added is the first thing you do. Use it for the task you would otherwise keep rehearsing.

            mm add "CS302"
          

          

            
Stack

            
For when something important is raised. Last thing you stacked is the thing you do now. Finish it, and the queue is where you left it.

            mm add -p "prod is down"
          

          

            
Quick

            
Small tasks that distract you if they stay in your head, and clutter the line if you mix them with real work. They wait until you flush them.

            mm add -q "email Sam"

A notes app, a stack of browser tabs, or a paper pile can hold tasks. They do not answer which one is the interruption, and they do not put you back. Getting Things Done has the same first move — get it out of your head — and then asks for projects, contexts, and a weekly review. mm stops at the three places above.


        
Why the shape is this

        
mm does not measure your focus, and it will not concentrate for you. It takes away two jobs your attention is bad at: holding a list, and choosing from that list while you are trying to work.

        

          

            
Working memory

            
A few items, then it spills.

            
People can keep about four chunks in mind at once. A task list is already too big for that. Nelson Cowan’s review of short-term memory put the limit near four, not the older “seven things” story. The queue is a place outside that limit.

          

          

            
Cognitive offloading

            
Write it down, then stop rehearsing it.

            
People already move memory into the world: notes, calendars, other people. Evan Risko and Sam Gilbert call that cognitive offloading. mm add is that move. The task is stored, so your attention can stay on the work.

          

          

            
Attention residue

            
A switch leaves part of you behind.

            
Sophie Leroy showed that when you leave a task unfinished, some attention stays on it and the next task suffers. The stack makes the switch explicit. mm done closes the interruption. The queue is the return address, so you are not reconstructing your place from memory.

          

          

            
Interruptions

            
Getting back is the expensive part.

            
Gloria Mark’s studies of office work found that after an interruption, returning to the original task took on the order of 23 minutes. A stack does not make the interruption free. It writes down what you were doing, and shows it again the moment the interruption is done.

          

        

        
One popular claim does not belong here. “You remember unfinished tasks better” (the Zeigarnik effect) has not held up as a general rule. A 2025 review found no reliable memory advantage for unfinished work. mm does not depend on that. It depends on the plainer facts above: the list does not fit in your head, writing it down frees attention, and a switch needs a way back.


        
Install

        
Python 3.11 or newer. Nothing to pip install.

        

          
git clone https://github.com/Toseef-Ahmad/mm.git
cd mm && ./install.sh

          
        

        
That writes ~/bin/mm and the manual, man mm. Your list is one file, ~/.mm/state.json. If an older mm left toml or habit files behind, delete ~/.mm and start empty.
